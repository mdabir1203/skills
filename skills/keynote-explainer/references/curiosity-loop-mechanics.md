# Curiosity-Loop Mechanics

The six neuromarketing drivers the skill leads with. Every scene must be tagged with one. No driver = the scene is cut. This is the deep dive on the spine from the main SKILL.md.

## Why this matters

Most motion videos fail not because they are ugly, but because they are inert. They look good; they do not do anything to the viewer's nervous system. The six drivers below are the levers that turn a video from "competent" to "world-class". They are also the difference between a video that the viewer watches once and a video they rewatch, share, and remember.

The drivers are not mutually exclusive. A single scene can use two. The concept must declare one as primary. The storyboard must tag every scene with at least one.

## 1. Open loop (Zeigarnik effect)

**The mechanism**: The human brain cannot tolerate incomplete information. A question that is planted and not answered creates a low-level anxiety that the viewer resolves by continuing to watch. The anxiety only resolves when the loop closes.

**How to use it**:
- Plant the loop in the first 3 seconds. The first frame is the question.
- Hint at the answer throughout the video — show pieces, but never the whole.
- Close the loop in the last 3 seconds. The closer is the resolution.
- Multiple loops are fine if they do not crowd. A primary loop + 1–2 secondary loops is the maximum.

**Examples**:
- "What if your inbox could reply itself?" — the loop is "show me". The video must show it.
- "The thinnest laptop we've ever made" — the loop is "how thin?". The video must reveal the dimension.
- "We rebuilt the wheel" — the loop is "what's different about your wheel?". The video must show the difference.

**Anti-patterns**:
- Loops that never close (the viewer feels cheated)
- Loops that close trivially ("the answer is X") — there was no real loop
- Loops that are not questions ("here's our new product") — that is a statement, not a loop

## 2. Pattern interrupt

**The mechanism**: Attention is a finite resource. The brain habituates to any stimulus in 5–7 seconds. A change in motion, color, scale, perspective, or audio resets the attention clock. Without pattern interrupts, the video loses 50% of the audience by second 12.

**How to use it**:
- Plan a pattern interrupt every 5–7 seconds. This is a hard rule, not a suggestion.
- The interrupt should be subtle enough to not jar, but distinct enough to reset attention.
- The most common interrupt: a hard cut to a different visual scale (close-up → wide shot, or vice versa).
- Audio interrupts (a music beat, a SFX, a VO line) count. Use them.
- The interrupt should be on a beat of the music, not arbitrary.

**Examples**:
- A product close-up that pulls back to reveal the full product in context
- A screen recording that cuts to a hand holding the device
- A wide shot that cuts to a macro detail
- A silent frame (no music) that breaks the rhythm
- A 1-frame color flash (slight white pulse) that signals a new beat

**Anti-patterns**:
- Interrupts that are too frequent (every 2 seconds) — feels chaotic
- Interrupts that are too rare (every 15+ seconds) — feels static
- Interrupts that are not on the music beat — feels arbitrary
- Interrupts that are the same every time — feels predictable

## 3. Mystery gap (Schwartz)

**The mechanism**: The viewer's imagination is more powerful than any visual you can show. When you hint at something deeper than what is on screen, the viewer's brain fills in the gap with their own curiosity. The gap is the marketing.

**How to use it**:
- Show a door; do not open it. The door is the mystery.
- Show a partial product, a silhouette, a backlit figure. The mind completes it.
- Use depth of field to put something in the foreground, out of focus, that the viewer wants to see clearly.
- Imply context that the video does not explain. "Designed in California" is not a fact; it is a mystery.
- Never close the mystery gap with explanation. The mystery is the point.

**Examples**:
- A backlit product reveal where the silhouette is visible but the surface is dark
- A close-up of a hand holding a device, with the screen content blurred
- A voiceover that says "we did not just rebuild this — we rethought it" without explaining how
- A wide shot of a factory, an office, a city at night — context without specificity

**Anti-patterns**:
- Mystery gaps that are too obvious (the viewer does not engage with the mystery)
- Mystery gaps that are explained (the magic is gone)
- Mystery gaps without payoff (the viewer feels cheated)

## 4. Specificity over abstraction

**The mechanism**: The brain processes specific, concrete information as more real and more trustworthy than abstract claims. "12 minutes 34 seconds" is real. "Lightning fast" is a claim. The viewer's nervous system responds to the first; the second triggers skepticism.

**How to use it**:
- Replace every abstract claim with a specific number, name, place, or detail.
- "Fast" → "in 1.2 seconds"
- "Powerful" → "8.2 teraflops"
- "Beautiful" → "the grain of brushed aluminum, finished by hand"
- "Everywhere" → "in 192 countries"
- "We" → a specific name ("Maria in Mexico City")

**Examples**:
- Apple: "the most advanced camera we've ever made" → show the camera's specific capabilities
- Google: "AI that helps you write" → show a specific email being drafted
- Stripe: "the future of payments" → show a specific transaction, with a specific merchant, in a specific country

**Anti-patterns**:
- "Revolutionary", "game-changing", "next-generation" — all banned as standalone claims
- "We are the best" — no specifics, no proof
- "AI-powered" without showing what the AI does

## 5. Contrast (delta)

**The mechanism**: The brain responds to changes, not absolutes. A loud sound after silence is louder than the same sound in noise. A bright color after dark is brighter. The contrast is the message.

**How to use it**:
- Structure scenes in pairs: before/after, expected/unexpected, simple/complex, silent/loud.
- Use the cut to deliver the contrast. A hard cut from quiet to loud is more powerful than a slow ramp.
- Visual contrast: dark frame → bright frame, static → motion, single element → many elements.
- Audio contrast: silence → music, music → silence, quiet → loud, voice → no voice.
- The contrast should serve the story, not be ornamental.

**Examples**:
- A frame of pure black before the product reveal
- A silent frame in the middle of a music-driven video
- A single word on screen for 2 seconds before the full sentence
- A close-up of a single detail before a wide shot of the whole product
- A still frame before a moving frame

**Anti-patterns**:
- Contrast that is too frequent (constant chaos)
- Contrast that is not on the music beat (feels arbitrary)
- Contrast that is the same in every scene (feels predictable)
- Contrast without intent (chaos for chaos's sake)

## 6. Endowed progress

**The mechanism**: Once someone feels they have started, they are far more likely to finish. "You are three steps in" is more compelling than "three steps to go". The closer the finish line appears, the harder they lean in.

**How to use it**:
- Frame the viewer's journey as already in progress. "Imagine you have been using this for a week" > "imagine you are using this for the first time".
- Show progress in the video itself. A loading bar that fills, a list that grows, a build that completes.
- Use the present tense. "You are building" not "you will build".
- Show the outcome as inevitable. "The question is not whether but when" > "you might want to try this".

**Examples**:
- "By the end of this video, you will know..." — the video is the journey, the viewer is at the start
- "You've been doing this wrong" — the viewer is already in the wrong state
- "Three things just changed" — the change has happened, the viewer is catching up
- A product demo where the user is shown succeeding — the viewer's brain mirrors it

**Anti-patterns**:
- Endowed progress that is dishonest (the viewer has not actually started)
- Endowed progress without payoff (the viewer feels they were tricked)
- Endowed progress that is too aggressive ("you are already losing money!") — feels manipulative

## Putting it together

A 30-second video should use ALL SIX drivers at least once. A 60-second video should use each at least twice. A 90-second video should use each at least three times. If a driver is not used, the video is missing a tool.

The six drivers are not techniques to sprinkle on. They are the structural skeleton of the video. The concept document declares which is primary. The storyboard tags each scene with one or more. The build executes them. The review pass checks that they all landed.

A video that hits all six drivers consistently is a video that the viewer cannot stop watching. A video that hits three is a video that the viewer watches. A video that hits one is a video that the viewer skips.
